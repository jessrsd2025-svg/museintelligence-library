## 1. What FIPS 180-2 Says — The Primary Source


The uploaded document is **FIPS Publication 180-2**, dated **August 1, 2002**, published by the **National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)** after approval by the **Secretary of Commerce**. It is the authoritative U.S. government standard that introduced SHA-256 to the world.


From the front matter:


> *“This Standard specifies four secure hash algorithms — SHA-1, SHA-256, SHA-384, and SHA-512 — for computing a condensed representation of electronic data (message).”*


> *“This standard supersedes FIPS 180-1, adding three algorithms that are capable of producing larger message digests. The SHA-1 algorithm specified herein is the same algorithm that was specified previously in FIPS 180-1.”*


From Section 1 (Introduction):


> *“This standard specifies four secure hash algorithms, SHA-1, SHA-256, SHA-384, and SHA-512. All four of the algorithms are iterative, one-way hash functions that can process a message to produce a condensed representation called a message digest.”*


From Section 6 (Applicability):


> *“This standard is applicable to all Federal departments and agencies for the protection of sensitive unclassified information…”*


> *“This standard shall be implemented whenever a secure hash algorithm is required for Federal applications, including use by other cryptographic algorithms and protocols.”*


### What FIPS 180-2 does NOT say


The document **does not name the NSA** as the designer. It identifies NIST as the **Maintenance Agency** (Section 5) and the **Secretary of Commerce** as the Approving Authority (Section 4). The algorithm is presented as a public standard with full mathematical specification — every constant, every rotation, every compression function is published in the clear.


This is the critical architectural fact: **the algorithm is fully specified.** There is no hidden component. The initial hash values for SHA-256 (Section 5.3.2) are derived from:


> *“the first thirty-two bits of the fractional parts of the square roots of the first eight prime numbers.”*

The sixty-four round constants (Section 4.2.2) are derived from:


> *“the first thirty-two bits of the fractional parts of the cube roots of the first sixty-four prime numbers.”*


These derivations are independently verifiable by anyone with a calculator. There are no “magic numbers” without explanation. This is the mathematical transparency that makes SHA-256 a **nothing-up-my-sleeve** construction.

The relationship is established by law. In practice, the NSA has historically designed cryptographic algorithms that NIST then standardizes for public use. This is the pattern for:


- **DES** (1977) — NSA modified IBM’s Lucifer design
