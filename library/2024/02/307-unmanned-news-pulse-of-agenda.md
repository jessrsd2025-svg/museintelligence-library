The PMF’s 13th brigade (Liwa al-Tafuf) was formed by splintered-off commanders from the Ali al-Sistani–loyalist PMF 11th brigade (Liwa Ali al-Akbar) in 2014. The new brigade was formed around a former head of security for the Karbala shrines named Qasim Muslih. Liwa al-Tafuf retained some support and drew some legitimacy from being connected to the shrines and to Sistani, but it quickly ceased to be a real “*atabat*” (shrine) PMF unit and instead fell increasingly under the administrative influence and operational control of [Kataib Hezbollah](https://www.washingtoninstitute.org/node/16641) founder and U.S.-designated terrorist Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis, then operational commander of the PMF.


The Popular Mobilization Forces (PMF) is a state-sponsored umbrella organization that includes various militia groups in Iraq. Some of these groups are aligned with Iran and have been involved in attacks against U.S. interests and personnel. One of these groups is the 13th brigade, also known as Liwa al-Tafuf, which has a complicated history and relationship with the PMF and its leadership.


Liwa al-Tafuf was formed by commanders who split from the 11th brigade (Liwa Ali al-Akbar), a PMF unit that was loyal to the influential Shia cleric Ali al-Sistani, who is widely respected by Iraqis for his moderate and pragmatic stance. The 11th brigade was established to protect the holy shrines in Karbala and Najaf, and to follow Sistani’s fatwa to fight against the Islamic State.


However, some of the commanders of the 11th brigade were dissatisfied with Sistani’s authority and influence over the PMF, and decided to form their own brigade, Liwa al-Tafuf, under the leadership of Qasim Muslih, a former security head for the Karbala shrines. Muslih was close to Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis, the founder of Kataib Hezbollah, a U.S.-designated terrorist group and one of the most powerful and radical factions within the PMF. Al-Muhandis was also the operational commander of the PMF until he was killed by a U.S. drone strike in January 2020, along with Iranian general Qasem Soleimani.


Under Muslih and al-Muhandis, Liwa al-Tafuf became more militant and aggressive, and adopted the ideology and tactics of Kataib Hezbollah. It participated in attacks against U.S. forces and facilities in Iraq, and supported Iran’s regional agenda. It also clashed with other PMF factions that were loyal to Sistani or the Iraqi government, such as the 9th brigade (Liwa Abbas al-Akbar) and the 1st brigade (Liwa al-Imam Ali).


The case of Liwa al-Tafuf illustrates the complex dynamics within the PMF, and the challenges that the Iraqi government faces in dealing with this heterogeneous and influential force. The PMF has played a crucial role in defeating the Islamic State and filling the security vacuum in many areas of Iraq, but it has also been a source of instability and violence, especially due to its Iranian-backed elements. The Iraqi government has tried to reform and integrate the PMF into the state security apparatus, but it has faced resistance and backlash from some of its leaders and supporters.


The future of Liwa al-Tafuf and other PMF factions will depend on several factors, such as the political situation in Iraq, the U.S.-Iran tensions, and the role of Sistani and other religious authorities. The PMF is not a monolithic entity, but a diverse and dynamic one that reflects the diversity and dynamism of Iraq itself.
