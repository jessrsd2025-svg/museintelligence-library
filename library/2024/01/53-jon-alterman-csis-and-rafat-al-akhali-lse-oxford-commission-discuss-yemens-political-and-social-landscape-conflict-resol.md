According to a report by RAND Corporation, Yemen’s civil war has its origins in political and economic grievances, which have been persistent challenges in Yemen’s modern history. Local grievances have undermined peace efforts, driving the unraveling of the Saudi-led coalition and contributing to the failure of the United Nations–led peace process.

Rafat Ali Al-Akhali is a strategic projects fellow at the Blavatnik School of Government. He is the Global Engagement Lead for the State Fragility initiative at the International Growth Centre and was previously Minister of Youth and Sports in the Government of Yemen. His professional background includes work in policy reforms, government development, and international consulting. Rafat holds master’s degrees in public policy and business administration, and has been actively involved in youth development and political inclusion. He is the co-founder and chairman of Resonate! Yemen and is involved in various foundations focused on youth engagement and entrepreneurship. Additionally, he was recognized as a Young Global Leader in 2015 by the World Economic Forum.


Jon B. [Alterman is a senior vice president, holds the Zbigniew Brzezinski Chair in Global Security and Geostrategy, and is director of the Middle East Program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS)](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman). Prior to joining CSIS in 2002, he served as a member of the Policy Planning Staff at the U.S. [Department of State and as a special assistant to the assistant secretary of state for Near Eastern affairs](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman). [He is a former international affairs fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, where he is now a life member](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman). He received his A.B. from Princeton University’s School of Public and International Affairs and his Ph.D. [in history from Harvard University](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman). He has lectured in more than 35 countries on five continents on subjects related to the Middle East and U.S. [policy toward the region](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman). [He is the author or coauthor of four books on the Middle East and the editor of five more](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman). [In addition to his policy work, he often teaches Middle Eastern studies at the Johns Hopkins School of Advanced International Studies and the George Washington University](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman). [His opinion pieces have appeared in the New York Times, Washington Post, Wall Street Journal, Financial Times, and other major publications](https://www.csis.org/people/jon-b-alterman).


According to the International Rescue Committee (IRC), the humanitarian crisis in Yemen has left millions of people in need of humanitarian aid. The UN’s 2023 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) for Yemen released on January 25 requests $4.3 billion to provide life-saving assistance to 17.3 million people, which is approximately 80 percent of the 21.6 million people identified by the UN as in need of humanitarian aid in 2023. The United States has provided nearly $4.5 billion to the Yemeni people since the crisis began more than seven years ago. UNICEF is on the ground to save children’s lives, help them cope with the impact of conflict, recover, and resume their childhoods. Conflict and violence have pushed more families into poverty and deprivation, and UNICEF is helping treat severe acute malnutrition in children by providing essential therapeutic food and medical supplies. Children are also receiving assistance with victim support and education on mines and explosive remnants of war. Furthermore, UNICEF and partners are rehabilitating damaged schools and establishing safe learning spaces.
