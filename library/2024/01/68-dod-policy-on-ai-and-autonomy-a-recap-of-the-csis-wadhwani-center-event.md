On January 9, 2024, the CSIS Wadhwani Center for AI and Advanced Technologies hosted a live-streamed event featuring Dr. Michael C. Horowitz, Professor of Political Science and the Associate Director of Perry World House at the University of Pennsylvania, and Gregory C. Allen, Adjunct Senior Fellow at the Center for a New American Security (CNAS). The event focused on the state of the DOD policy towards AI and autonomy, and how it affects the future of defense planning and international cooperation.


Dr. Michael C. Horowitz is the Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Force Development and Emerging Capabilities in the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy. He has over 20 years of experience in leadership, research, and academic roles focused on international security issues. He is currently on a leave of absence from the University of Pennsylvania, where he served as Director of Perry World House and Richard Perry Professor. He is the author of The Diffusion of Military Power: Causes and Consequences for International Politics, and the co-author of Why Leaders Fight. He won the Karl Deutsch Award given by the International Studies Association for early career contributions to the fields of international relations and peace research. He has published in a wide array of peer reviewed journals and popular outlets. His expertise includes defense innovation, the intersection of emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence and robotics with global politics, the role of leaders in international politics, and geopolitical forecasting methodology1. He previously served in the Pentagon, working on force development issues in the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy. He is a life member at the Council on Foreign Relations.


Gregory C. Allen is the director of the Wadhwani Center for AI and Advanced Technologies at the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS)3. Prior to joining CSIS, he was the director of strategy and policy at the Department of Defense (DOD) Joint Artificial Intelligence Center, where he oversaw development and implementation of the DOD’s AI Strategy, drove policy and human capital reforms to accelerate the DOD’s adoption of AI, and developed mechanisms for AI governance and ethics. Mr. Allen’s expertise and professional experience spans AI, robotics, semiconductors, space technology, and national security. Prior to working at the DOD, he was the head of market analysis and competitive strategy at Blue Origin, a space technology manufacturer and space launch services provider. He holds a joint MPP/MBA degree from the Harvard Kennedy School of Government and the Harvard Business School. Mr. Allen’s writing and commentary has appeared in the New York Times, the Washington Post, The Economist, Nature, CNN, Fox News, Foreign Policy, and WIRED.


DoD policy 3000.09 is a directive that establishes policy and assigns responsibilities for developing and using autonomous and semi-autonomous functions in weapon systems, including armed platforms that are remotely operated or operated by onboard personnel. The policy aims to minimize the probability and consequences of failures in autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems that could lead to unintended engagements. The policy requires that autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems will be designed to allow commanders and operators to exercise appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force, and that persons who authorize the use of, direct the use of, or operate such systems will do so with appropriate care and in accordance with the law of war, applicable treaties, weapon system safety rules, and applicable rules of engagement. The policy also stipulates that the weapon system has demonstrated appropriate performance, capability, reliability, effectiveness, and suitability under realistic conditions, and that the design, development, deployment, and use of systems incorporating AI capabilities is consistent with the DoD AI Ethical Principles and the DoD Responsible AI (RAI) Strategy and Implementation Pathway


Dr. Horowitz and Mr. Allen discussed the role of the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy (OUSD(P)) in shaping the future force, and how it coordinates with other DOD components, such as the Joint Artificial Intelligence Center (JAIC) and the Defense Innovation Unit (DIU), to ensure alignment of AI and autonomy initiatives with strategic objectives and operational needs. The JAIC is the DOD’s focal point for accelerating the adoption and integration of AI across all domains of warfare. It is responsible for developing and implementing AI standards, tools, platforms, frameworks, and governance models for the DOD. The DIU is an organization that connects the DOD with commercial technology companies to accelerate the delivery of innovative solutions to national security challenges.


They also explored how the OUSD(P) collaborates with allies and partners on AI and autonomy policy, emphasizing the significance of the Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of Artificial Intelligence and Autonomy, which was signed by 13 countries in November 2023.


The Political Declaration is a landmark document that establishes a common vision and principles for the responsible military use of AI and autonomy among like-minded nations. It reaffirms the importance of human control, adherence to the law of war, ethical standards, trust, transparency, and international cooperation in the development and deployment of AI and autonomy systems. Dr. Horowitz and Mr. Allen highlighted how the Declaration can serve as a foundation for further dialogue and coordination on AI and autonomy issues, such as interoperability, verification, accountability, and risk management.


Additionally, Dr. Horowitz and Mr. Allen touched upon the DOD’s policy on Humanitarian Assistance (HA), which is a security cooperation activity that aims to alleviate human suffering caused by natural or man-made disasters, or to support efforts to promote human rights, democracy, or civil society. They explained how HA is funded through the Overseas Humanitarian, Disaster, and Civic Aid (OHDACA) appropriation, which provides flexible and responsive funding for DOD to conduct HA activities in support of U.S. foreign policy objectives.


Moreover, Dr. Horowitz and Mr. Allen examined how AI and autonomy applications can enhance HA activities, such as disaster relief, medical assistance, infrastructure repair, mine clearance, and education. They cited examples of how DOD has leveraged AI and autonomy technologies to improve situational awareness, decision making, logistics, coordination, and communication in HA scenarios. They also acknowledged the challenges and risks associated with using AI and autonomy in HA contexts, such as ethical dilemmas, legal implications, social impacts, technical limitations, and security threats.


The event was a stimulating and informative discussion that sheds light on the current state and future direction of DOD policy towards AI and autonomy, as well as its implications for defense planning and international cooperation.
