The national security launch market is a significant multibillion-dollar procurement of launch services scheduled for 2025 through 2029, with a focus on the role of the U.S. Space Force in selecting providers of national security launch services. The Space Force is responsible for ensuring the access to space for critical missions such as intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance, missile warning, communications, and navigation. To achieve this goal, the Space Force has launched several initiatives, such as the Space Domain Awareness Marketplace, the SSC Front Door, and SpaceWERX, designed to facilitate the procurement process and foster innovation and collaboration among government and industry partners.


One of the companies that is vying for a share of the national security launch market is VOX Space, a subsidiary of Virgin Orbit, dedicated to providing responsive space capabilities to the national security and intelligence communities. VOX Space offers launch services utilizing Virgin Orbit’s LauncherOne rocket capable of delivering payloads to low Earth orbit (LEO). LauncherOne has a unique approach of air-launching the rocket from a modified Boeing 747 aircraft, offering increased flexibility in launch location and timing. This enables VOX Space to meet the needs of customers who require rapid and responsive access to space.


However, VOX Space is not the only company involved in providing launch services for national security missions. The market is highly competitive and diverse, with several other players offering different capabilities and features. Some of the notable companies include:


– United Launch Alliance (ULA), a joint venture between Boeing and Lockheed Martin, which operates the Atlas V and Delta IV Heavy rockets, and is developing the Vulcan Centaur rocket for future missions.


– SpaceX, founded by Elon Musk, which operates the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets, and is developing the Starship/Super Heavy vehicle for lunar and Mars missions.


– Northrop Grumman Innovation Systems, formerly Orbital ATK, which operates the Antares and Pegasus rockets, and is developing the OmegA rocket for medium to heavy lift missions.


– Rocket Lab USA, a U.S.-based subsidiary of Rocket Lab, a New Zealand company, which operates the Electron rocket for small satellite missions.


– Aevum, a startup company that uses autonomous technologies to launch small satellites from conventional airports using a reusable air-launch system called Ravn X.


– Firefly Black, a division of Firefly Aerospace, which offers dedicated launch services for small satellites using the Alpha rocket.


– X-Bow Launch Systems, a startup company that uses crossbow-like launchers to catapult small satellites into orbit.


The national security launch market is not only competitive but also responsive. The Space Force and its industry partners are embracing the concept of tactically responsive space as a comprehensive approach to delivering space capabilities to the warfighter within tactically relevant timelines. This approach involves rapid acquisition, development, testing, and operation of space systems, with a focus on their capacity to respond to emerging threats and opportunities. Some of the examples of tactically responsive space include:


– The Rapid Agile Launch Initiative (RALI), a program that awards contracts to commercial launch providers within days or weeks of payload availability.


– The Space Enterprise Consortium (SpEC), an organization that facilitates collaboration between government and industry stakeholders to develop innovative space technologies using streamlined acquisition processes.


– The Blackjack program, a project that aims to demonstrate a global constellation of low-cost satellites that can provide persistent overhead coverage and resilient communications.


The national security launch market is a dynamic and evolving sector that reflects the changing needs and challenges of the U.S. space enterprise. The Space Force plays a key role in selecting providers of national security launch services that can offer reliable, affordable, and responsive access to space. The market is also driven by innovation and competition among various companies that offer diverse capabilities and features for different types of missions. The market is also influenced by the concept of tactically responsive space that seeks to deliver space capabilities to the warfighter within tactically relevant timelines.

The Space Force and NASA are two distinct entities that have different roles and responsibilities in the U.S. space enterprise. While both organizations are involved in exploring and utilizing space for various purposes, they have fundamental differences in their missions, goals, cultures, and approaches.


The Space Force is a military service branch under the Department of Defense that was established in 2019. Its mission is to organize, train, equip, and maintain space forces in order to protect U.S. interests in space and provide space capabilities to joint military operations. The Space Force is responsible for ensuring freedom of action in, from, and to space, and providing combat-ready space forces that enable the joint force. The Space Force operates various space systems, such as satellites, launch vehicles, ground stations, and command and control networks, that support missions such as intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance, missile warning, communications, navigation, and space domain awareness. The Space Force also conducts space operations, such as launch, on-orbit maneuver, rendezvous and proximity operations, reentry, and recovery, that enable the deployment, sustainment, and disposal of space assets. The Space Force also develops and acquires new space technologies and capabilities that enhance its readiness and effectiveness.


NASA is a civilian agency under the executive branch that was established in 1958. Its mission is to lead the nation in scientific discovery and exploration of space for the benefit of humanity. NASA is responsible for advancing the understanding of Earth, the solar system, and the universe through scientific research and exploration. NASA also develops and operates various space systems, such as spacecraft, rockets, rovers, landers, orbiters, telescopes, and stations, that enable scientific investigations and human exploration of space. NASA also conducts space missions, such as planetary exploration, lunar landing, asteroid redirection, Mars sample return, and heliophysics observation, that expand the frontiers of knowledge and inspire future generations. NASA also collaborates with other government agencies, industry partners, academic institutions, and international organizations to leverage resources and expertise for its endeavors.


The Space Force and NASA have different missions, goals, cultures, and approaches in their involvement in space. The Space Force is a military organization that focuses on protecting U.S. interests in space and providing space capabilities to joint military operations. NASA is a civilian organization that focuses on advancing scientific discovery and exploration of space for the benefit of humanity. While both organizations share a common interest in ensuring a safe and sustainable use of space for peaceful purposes, they have different perspectives and priorities in their activities and operations in space.


In Memoriam: Mark Baird, a Pioneer of Tactically Responsive Space


![](https://jessrsdincyahoocom.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/spacenews.jpeg?w=259)


Mark Baird, was a retired U.S. Air Force brigadier general and a distinguished leader in the space community. He died on November 30, 2023, at the age of 67.


Mark Baird had a remarkable career that spanned over three decades of service in the U.S. Air Force and the intelligence community. He held various key positions, such as the deputy director of the Space Force Planning Task Force, the deputy director of the National Reconnaissance Office, the director of space programs in the Office of the Assistant Secretary of the Air Force for Acquisition, and the commander of the 50th Space Wing.


He was widely respected for his visionary and transformative leadership in the space enterprise, especially in advancing the concept and practice of tactically responsive space. He championed the idea of rapidly developing and deploying space capabilities to meet emerging threats and operational needs. He oversaw several successful missions that demonstrated the feasibility and utility of this approach, such as Operationally Responsive Space-1, TacSat-3, and ORS-5.


He also played a pivotal role in shaping the future of the U.S. Space Force, as he helped develop the strategic vision, organizational structure, and operational doctrine for the new service branch. He was instrumental in establishing the Space Rapid Capabilities Office, which aims to accelerate the delivery of innovative space solutions to warfighters.


Mark Baird was a true pioneer and a dedicated public servant who made lasting contributions to our national security and space exploration. He will be dearly missed by his family, friends, colleagues, and all who knew him. We honor his legacy and express our deepest gratitude for his service.

The **VICTUS NOX program** is a **responsive space demonstration mission** that aims to demonstrate the end-to-end tactically responsive space capability, including the launch segment, space segment, ground segment, and on-orbit operations. [The program was awarded to **Firefly Aerospace** as part of the Tactically Responsive Space Launch Service Task Order under the Orbital Services Program 4 Contract](https://spacenews.com/firefly-launches-space-force-victus-nox-mission/). [The mission was launched on **September 14, 2023**, using Firefly’s Alpha rocket with just **24-hour notice**](https://spacenews.com/firefly-launches-space-force-victus-nox-mission/). [The mission was designed to show that the United States can put a satellite in space fast if there’s ever a national security issue](https://spacenews.com/firefly-launches-space-force-victus-nox-mission/).

*In September 2023, a new record was set in space launch. Just 27 hours after receiving an order to launch, a team comprised of U.S. Space Systems Command, Boeing’s Millennium Space Systems (which built the satellite) and Firefly Aerospace (which built the rocket) successfully launched a satellite into low Earth orbit. Named VICTUS NOX, the mission aimed to demonstrate the ability to launch in a timeframe much shorter than typical national security space launches. It also highlighted the need for a holistic approach to tactically responsive space, including in acquisition, infrastructure, processes, and culture.*
