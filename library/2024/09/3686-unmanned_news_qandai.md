- The Maritime Security Dialogue is a public speaker series focused on maritime security issues, co-led by CSIS and the U.S. Naval Institute.
- **Focus Areas**: It addresses challenges facing the Navy, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard, including national maritime policy, naval concept development, and program design.
- **Context**: The discussions consider budgetary challenges, technological opportunities, and strategic adjustments affecting U.S. maritime forces over the next 10-15 years.

The **NDAA** is crucial for providing the legal framework and securing funding for the Department of Defense. The Maritime Security Dialogue is a collaborative event series hosted by the CSIS International Security Program (ISP) and the U.S. Naval Institute. This particular event features Senator Mark Kelly (D-AZ) and Congressman Mike Waltz (R-FL), who will discuss the importance of rebuilding America’s maritime sector to address the rising threat from China. The dialogue will be moderated by Dr. Seth G. Jones, CSIS senior vice president and director of the ISP, with opening remarks by RADM Raymond A. Spicer, USN (Ret.)1.
The series aims to address the unique challenges faced by the Navy, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard, covering topics from national maritime policy to naval concept development and program design. Given the current budgetary challenges, technological advancements, and strategic adjustments, the U.S. maritime forces are expected to undergo significant changes over the next decade1.
If you’re interested in more details or want to watch the event, you can visit the CSIS website or follow them on their social media channels1.

US and China: Comparing their navies the old way – The Conversation

A traditional way of assessing the balance of naval power is to count and compare the number of warships operated by each country. Even on that metric, the US isn’t outgunned by China, based on recent data.
China is frequently described as the world’s largest navy. But the US has more of the most important types of major warships, which are suitable for maritime warfare. The count only shifts in China’s favour for lighter and less heavily armed ships, such as frigates and coastal patrol vessels.
China’s advantage in lighter classes of warships could be particularly important in a conflict contained largely within the Taiwan Strait and other coastal areas near China.[https://theconversation.com/the-us-navy-is-still-more-powerful-than-chinas-more-so-than-the-australian-government-is-letting-on-208466?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=bylinelinkedinbutton](https://theconversation.com/the-us-navy-is-still-more-powerful-than-chinas-more-so-than-the-australian-government-is-letting-on-208466?utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=bylinelinkedinbutton)

# Using Artificial Intelligence to Combat Wildlife Crime


![Portrait of John Matuszewski](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/styles/square/public/media/uploads/images/Me.jpg)


By [John Matuszewski](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/person/john-matuszewski) on *September 12, 2024*


Wildlife crime is costly, not only for ecosystem conservation but also for local and national economies. Traditional methods of combating wildlife crime include regular patrols and camera traps, but these are also costly in manpower and resources. New tools using artificial intelligence are advancing the fight against wildlife crime. Applications of machine learning have become a powerful tool for detection and identification both of the wildlife they seek to protect and of crimes being committed.[https://www.wilsoncenter.org/program/science-and-technology-innovation-program](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/program/science-and-technology-innovation-program)
