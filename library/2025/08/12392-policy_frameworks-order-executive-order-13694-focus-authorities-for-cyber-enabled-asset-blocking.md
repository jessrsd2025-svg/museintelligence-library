### 2. Methods Used to Assess Risk Scores for Wallets


Wallet risk assessment on TRON generally employs advanced blockchain analytics provided by forensic partners such as TRM Labs, Chainalysis, and Scorechain:


- **On-chain behavioral patterns:** Frequency, transaction graph structure, wallet churn, mixing/layering, connection to known blacklisted (SDN) wallets.
- **Linkage Analysis:** Detects direct/indirect transactional proximity to flagged DTO/FTO actors (e.g., Cartel de los Soles, Hezbollah).
- **Exposure Metrics:** Percentage of volume linked to mixers, DEX off-ramps, burner wallets, and sanctioned/previously frozen addresses.
- **Automated Scoring:** Tools like Scorechain compute a dynamic risk score (e.g., 0.94 = ‘Very High Risk’), integrating typology rules, historical data, anomaly detection, and corroborating with external SDN (OFAC Specially Designated Nationals) and FinCEN watchlists.
- **Augmentation with Off-chain Intelligence:** Cross-matches wallet KYC leaks, dark web forums, reported scam vectors, and transactional timing with major enforcement actions or embargo events.

**Examples of Sanctions Evasion Tactics Used by Russia (Targeted by E.O. 14024):**


Russia has employed various tactics to circumvent Western sanctions, particularly those imposed after its invasion of Ukraine. E.O. 14024 targets these tactics, including:


- **Use of Third-Party Intermediaries:** Russia often relies on third countries, shell companies, and intermediaries to route transactions, conceal the true nature or ownership of goods, and disguise the end-user—especially for dual-use and military goods.
- **Transshipment through Neighboring Countries:** Goods such as high-tech components are shipped to countries in Central Asia, the Caucasus, or the Middle East and then reexported to Russia.
- **False Documentation:** Exporters may falsify paperwork regarding the origin, destination, or end-user of materials and technologies, making it harder to track illicit transfers.
- **Use of Alternative Payment Mechanisms:** Russia employs non-USD currencies, cryptocurrency, bartering, and other payment systems to avoid detection through the global financial system.
- **Acquisition through Unofficial Channels:** Procuring critical goods via gray markets, brokers, or illicit networks that do not perform due diligence on sanctions compliance.
- **Shipping Evasion Tactics:** Ships may disable transponders (AIS cloaking), conduct ship-to-ship transfers, or falsify cargo manifests to avoid scrutiny.


E.O. 14024 explicitly allows the U.S. to sanction persons—individuals and entities—involved in these types of evasion or deceptive practices.

**2. How E.O. 14024 Targets the Tech and Defense Sectors:**


E.O. 14024 authorizes the Treasury and State Departments to designate and block property and interests in property of persons operating in sectors of the Russian economy—including the **technology** and **defense** sectors. Here’s how:


- **Designation of Key Entities:** Leading Russian technology and defense companies are added to the Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) list, freezing their assets under U.S. jurisdiction and prohibiting U.S. persons from dealing with them.
- **Sectoral Sanctions Identifications:** The Order allows U.S. authorities to specifically name sectors—such as technology, defense, aerospace, electronics, and marine—as subject to sanctions. This means any entity determined to operate within these sectors can be targeted.
- **Secondary Sanctions Risk:** Non-U.S. persons—such as foreign suppliers of chips or dual-use parts—may be sanctioned for materially supporting or supplying Russia’s sanctioned tech and defense sectors. This is intended to cut off access to critical technologies for Russia’s military-industrial complex.
- **Targeting Supply Chains:** Sanctions are not limited to end users; companies and individuals that facilitate supply chains for Russia’s tech and defense sectors—even if not based in Russia—can face blocking sanctions.


**Examples:**


- Companies that manufacture or supply semiconductors, software, or critical microelectronics to Russian defense firms may be sanctioned.
- Entities providing inputs, maintenance, or technical support to the Russian defense industry are at risk.

**3. How General Licenses Modify the Impact of E.O. 14024:**


**General Licenses (GLs)** are authorizations issued by the U.S. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) that allow certain transactions involving sanctioned persons or sectors, which would otherwise be prohibited under E.O. 14024.


**Impact-modifying functions of General Licenses:**


- **Humanitarian & Civilian Exceptions:** Allow U.S. and non-U.S. persons to engage in transactions necessary for food, medicine, and medical device trade—even with sanctioned entities.
- **Wind-down Periods:** Provide a limited period (e.g., 30 or 60 days) for U.S. persons to divest from, or wind-down operations with, newly sanctioned entities, reducing unintended business disruption.
- **Maintenance of Key Infrastructure:** Permit transactions necessary to maintain systems like energy grids, IT infrastructure, or communications, which indirectly involve sanctioned parties but are critical to global or domestic stability.
- **Diplomatic and International Organization Exemptions:** Allow the U.S. government, international organizations, and their partners to conduct necessary diplomatic or peacekeeping functions.
- **Averting Overbreadth:** GLs can limit the scope of E.O. 14024 by ensuring that essential transactions unrelated to the Russian government or its malign activities remain permissible despite broad sanctions.


**Example:**


- GLs have allowed for continued payment of taxes, utility fees, or permits to Russian government agencies by non-Russian companies operating in Russia, providing time for orderly exit.

## Common Russian Sanctions Evasion Tactics Targeted by E.O. 14024


**1. Third-Party Intermediaries:**


- *What:* Russia uses shell companies and cutouts in third countries to route transactions and obscure true beneficiaries.
- *Example:* Tech components are billed to entities in Turkey, UAE, or Central Asia, then quietly reexported into Russia.


**2. Transshipment through Neighboring Countries:**


- *What:* Sensitive goods, including high technology, are first shipped to Armenia, Kazakhstan, Georgia, UAE, Turkey, or China, then re-exported to Russia.
- *Example:* Dual-use electronics routed through Kazakhstan with paperwork falsely showing local end-use.


**3. Falsified Documents:**


- *What:* Exporters create false customs or end-user documents to hide actual destinations, users, or nature of goods.
- *Example:* Microchips labeled as for “consumer electronics” in a third country but diverted to Russia’s defense sector.


**4. Alternative Payment Mechanisms:**


- *What:* Use of non-USD currencies, cryptocurrencies, bartering (goods-for-goods), or non-bank financial systems to avoid SWIFT monitoring.
- *Example:* Settling oil trades in yuan, crypto rails, or barter transactions with Iran and China.


**5. Unofficial/Gray Markets:**


- *What:* Critical goods are sourced via underground brokers or markets that operate outside official compliance oversight.
- *Example:* Middlemen in Dubai or Hong Kong procuring sanctioned parts via gray market suppliers.


**6. Shipping Evasion:**


- *What:* Russian-linked vessels disable tracking (AIS cloaking), do ship-to-ship transfers on the high seas, or misreport cargo.
- *Example:* Russian oil transferred between tankers near Greece, with both ships disabling transponders.

## How E.O. 14024 Targets Tech & Defense Sectors


- Authorizes Treasury/State to SDN-list (freeze & block) any entity or individual operating in Russia’s tech, defense, aerospace, electronics, and marine sectors.
- Applies “sectoral” sanctions: any person/entity (not just Russian) supplying dual-use or defense-related hardware/software to these sectors can also be sanctioned (“secondary sanctions”).
- **Supply Chains Targeted:** Not just direct sales but *maintenance, supply, or facilitation* of Russian defense/tech ops can be designated (even outside Russia).


*Example:*


- Semiconductor maker in Asia selling to a front company in the UAE, knowing the chips will end up in Russian drones, could be sanctioned under E.O. 14024.


---


## How General Licenses (GLs) Modify E.O. 14024 Sanctions


- **Humanitarian Exceptions:** GLs allow transactions for food, medicines, and medical devices even if the counterparty is sanctioned.
- **“Wind-down” Periods:** Temporary licenses allow companies to legally exit or divest from newly sanctioned ties.
- **Infrastructure Exception:** Essential transactions (tax, utilities, IT) are permitted to avoid humanitarian or critical infrastructure crises.
- **Diplomatic/International Orgs:** Routine government and international body transactions are generally protected.
- **Narrowing Overbreadth:** Limits unintended consequences (e.g., utility payments by a U.S.-owned business in Russia).


*Example:* OFAC GLs have allowed non-Russian companies to pay utility bills, taxes, or settle contracts for a limited period while winding down Russian business after new sanctions.
