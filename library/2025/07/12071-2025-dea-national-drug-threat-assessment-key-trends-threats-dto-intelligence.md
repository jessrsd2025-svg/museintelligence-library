## 1. Executive Overview


- **Mexican cartels remain the #1 strategic threat** to US public health, safety, and national security.
- Eight major Mexican and international DTOs have now been designated as **Foreign Terrorist Organizations (FTOs)/Specially Designated Global Terrorists (SDGTs)** including Sinaloa, CJNG, Gulf, Northeast, New Michoacán Family, United Cartels, Tren de Aragua (Venezuela), and MS-13.
- Nationally, **overdose deaths are finally declining** for the first time in years—but at over 84,000 in 2024, opioids, primarily fentanyl, still kill more Americans than any other drug.
- DTOs leverage:
  - **Synthetic innovation** (fentanyl, nitazenes, xylazine, medetomidine)
  - **Diversified transportation** (ports, tunnels, interstate highways)
  - **U.S. distribution partnerships** (gangs, street-level networks)
  - **Money laundering innovation** (cryptocurrency, Chinese networks, real estate, trade fraud)

## 2. Top Line Cartel Threats (2025)


### Sinaloa Cartel (CDS)


- *Global footprint:* Operates in >40 countries, decentralized distribution, dominant in CA/AZ border zones but present in almost all US states.
- *Key developments:*
  - **Leadership arrests (2024):** Ismael “El Mayo” Zambada-Garcia and Joaquín Guzmán-López.
  - **Extradition of Rafael Caro Quintero (2025):** Symbolic closure for the murder of DEA agent Enrique “Kiki” Camarena.
- *Product innovation:*
  - Fentanyl, meth (97% purity), heroin, cocaine.
  - *Drug cocktails:* Fentanyl/xylazine/other.
  - Use of social media/encrypted apps for sales and crew recruitment.
- *Criminal diversification:* Extortion, kidnapping, stolen petroleum (“black market oil”), environmental crimes.


---


### CJNG (Jalisco New Generation Cartel)


- *Strategic alliances:* Expanding influence in both Mexico and >40 countries.
- *Unique structure:* Franchise-based (licenses local cells); finances via Los Cuinis, extensive money laundering.
- *Core drugs:* Fentanyl, meth, cocaine.
- *Diversification:* Gasoline theft, real estate fraud, human smuggling taxation.


---


### Other FTOs


- **Northeast Cartel (CDN):** Key in S. Texas; notorious for violence, human smuggling, links to Sinaloa.
- **Familia Michoacána & New Michoacán Family:** Controlling port access for precursor chemicals; heavy alliances and conflicts with larger cartels for control of trafficking routes.
- **United Cartels (CU):** Defensive, territory-based alliance.
- **Gulf Cartel (CDG):** Fragmented, control over narcotics/human smuggling across the Rio Grande, partnership with CJNG.


---


### New FTOs/Gangs


- **Tren de Aragua (Venezuela):** Migrant extortion, violent crime, and tusi trafficking.
- **MS-13:** Transnational gang with major US footprint, involved in drug distribution and severe violence.

## 3. Drug Innovation & Market Dynamics


### Fentanyl


- *Key statistic:* 74,702 deaths involved fentanyl in 2023—2/3 of all fatal ODs.
- *Mixing trends:* Increasing prevalence of xylazine (non-opioid sedative; “tranq dope”) making ODs deadlier and harder to reverse.
- *Fake pharmaceutical pills:* Widespread, visually indistinct from legitimate oxycodone.


### Nitazenes


- 17 types detected across the US—often more potent than fentanyl, pushed as “designer synthetics”; often mixed with fentanyl or heroin.


### Xylazine & Medetomidine


- *Tranq/dope:* Xylazine now top fentanyl adulterant, found in every state; medetomidine emerging.
- *Major risk:* Not reversed by naloxone, causes severe soft tissue damage.


### Methamphetamine


- *Production:* Near-industrial scale in Mexico; >95% purity.
- *US meth labs:* Disappearing—market almost completely “owned” by Mexican TCOs.
- *Pill innovation:* Fake “Adderall” and “MDMA” pills with meth (risking psychostimulant ODs).


### Heroin & Cocaine


- *Heroin market share:* Steadily declining—transitional replacement by fentanyl evident in deaths and seizures (82% of heroin deaths in 2024 involved fentanyl).
- *Cocaine:* Still at crisis/high levels, especially adulterated with fentanyl for “super speedball” effect.
- *US seizures:* 63 metric tons in 2024, most at CA-Mexico border.


### Marijuana


- Majority of “legal” state excess production controlled by Asian and Mexican TCOs.
- *Chinese organized crime now dominates illicit grow operations* in legal/decriminalized states; money laundering via real estate, underground banking, and straw owners.

## 4. DTO Adaptation, Economic Power & Illicit Finance


- **Money laundering:**
  - Advanced use of *Chinese Underground Banking, Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML),* and *cryptocurrency*: Over $2.5B in virtual currency seizures (2020–2024).
  - DTOs shift methods rapidly—traditional bulk cash movement is down, virtual laundering up.
- **Black market oil/gas trafficking:**
  - Cartels (especially CJNG, CDS) steal and smuggle Mexican PEMEX oil, selling to US firms.
- **Synthetic innovation => market resilience:**
  - Short disruption from precursor regulation—shift to “designer” reagents, new brokering routes, and continuous synthetic creativity.


---


## 5. Public Health & Security Impact


- **OD deaths down for the first time,** but plateauing at extremely high levels.
  - Major drivers: Synthetic opioids (fentanyl, nitazenes), psychostimulants, cocaine, xylazine adulteration.
- **Drug cocktails** now the market norm.
  - 1 in 4 cocaine submissions; 1 in 8 meth samples contain fentanyl.
- **Youth impact**: Potency and marketing of THC products, fake pills, and flavored vapes have led to record youth use and unintentional poisonings.
- **Tribal/Indian Country:**
  - Meth and marijuana trafficked with unique geographic/sovereign challenges. *Operation Overdrive* highlighted as a model of federal-tribal-local collaboration (e.g., Blackfeet Reservation, Montana fentanyl crisis).


---


## 6. Geospatial and Market Network Intelligence


- **Border hotspots:**
  - CA, AZ primarily for fentanyl, meth, cocaine; East Texas for marijuana.
  - Interstate highways (I-10, I-40, I-35, I-70, I-95) as *critical supply distribution arteries*.
- **Cartel Operations “At-a-Glance”:**
  - CDS, CJNG: Present in virtually every state, “hub-and-spoke” distribution to gangs, independent traffickers.
  - Violence, money laundering, and cross-border criminal convergence (drugs, arms, oil, human trafficking).
- **Asian TCOs:**
  - Now integral to marijuana, chemical precursor, and money laundering supply chains in the US.


---


## 7. Law Enforcement & Policy


- **Major Events of 2024–2025:**
  - *Extradition of 29 cartel leaders, including Caro Quintero*: Milestone, but DTOs remain adaptive and decentralized.
  - Ongoing multi-agency coordinated strikes and designations—*FTO status* heightens law enforcement and regulatory tools.
- **Operational emphasis:**
  - Multi-jurisdictional task forces, money laundering prosecution, disruption of virtual and trade-based laundering channels, and increased cooperation with international partners (especially Mexico, Canada).


---


## 8. Key Analytical Takeaways


- While the **cartels continue to innovate and adapt rapidly,** supply chain interdiction and enforcement action (major arrests, chemical precursor controls) can cause only temporary disruptions unless bolstered by broader financial, technological, and public health strategies.
- *Drug supply reduction alone will not end the crisis*: Demand, structural market adaptation, new psychoactive substances, and money laundering resilience ensure market continuity.
- **Synthetic evolution is the greatest challenge:** As federal controls zero in on one compound, TCOs shift to another—necessitating scientific, forensic, and regulatory agility.
- **True threat is convergence:** Drug trafficking, violence, illicit finance, environmental crime, and public corruption are connected. Countermeasures must be equally wide-ranging.
- *Public health victory (declining OD deaths) is fragile*: Requires continuous innovation in both supply and demand reduction, naloxone access, education, and cross-border intelligence.

Deep Dive: FBI Decentralized Reporting & Local Seizure Databases


## Overview of UCR Decentralized Reporting


Local, state, tribal, and federal agencies voluntarily submit crime and seizure data to the FBI’s Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) Program either through coordinated state programs or directly to the FBI. This decentralized model ensures broad participation—over 18,000 agencies—but leads to varying data completeness and reporting timelines across jurisdictions.


Agencies can:


- Submit summary totals or detailed incident data via the National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS)
- Continue legacy reporting through state UCR offices or transition directly to the FBI’s Crime Data Explorer (CDE) portal
- Leverage automated Record Management Systems (RMS) that feed into NIBRS or the National Data Exchange (N-DEx) for more granular incident information3.


## National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS)


- Captures up to 52 offense categories per incident, including drug-seizure details (type, quantity, method of concealment)
- Provides context: victim/offender demographics, location, time, property values, and whether a computer was involved
- Phased from Summary Reporting System (SRS) to NIBRS-only as of January 1, 2021, enhancing analytic specificity but still requiring agency-level transition support.


## Crime Data Explorer & Real-Time Access


The Crime Data Explorer (CDE) web interface offers quarterly UCR releases, interactive visualizations, and CSV downloads. Users can drill down to county and agency levels, chart drug-seizure trends, and compare jurisdictions over time.


## Local Seizure Databases & N-DEx Integration


Most law enforcement agencies maintain local “seizure databases” within their RMS to track narcotics, weapons, currency, and asset forfeiture. The FBI’s N-DEx platform aggregates these records to enable:


- Cross-jurisdiction link-analysis of seizure patterns (e.g., identifying recurring courier profiles)
- Geospatial mapping of drug-interdiction hotspots
- Automated alerts when new seizure records match ongoing investigations elsewhere
- Enriched situational awareness by fusing SDS, NCIC, and local RMS entries into a unified interface.


| Feature | Local RMS Seizure Logs | N-DEx Aggregation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data Scope | Individual agency seizures | Nationwide incident, arrest, and booking records |
| Access | Internal only | Authorized multi-agency web portal |
| Linking | Manual cross-checks | Automated link-analysis and network visualization |
| Timeliness | Varies by agency processing time | Near real-time updates contingent on agency submission |
| Data Types | Drug types, weights, forfeitures | Plus photos, narrative reports, field contacts |


# Deep Dive: CDC WONDER Provisional Overdose Mortality Query


## CDC WONDER Platform & Data Sources


CDC WONDER is an online query system for public-health statistics, including provisional and final mortality data, census population estimates, and environmental metrics. The “Multiple Cause of Death” module hosts provisional death counts by jurisdiction, cause, and demographics6.


## Provisional Overdose Mortality Module


- **Data Coverage:** Deaths occurring within the 50 states and DC as of the query date; excludes Puerto Rico for national totals
- **Timeframe:** Monthly updates with 12-month rolling ending periods (e.g., Jan 2024 – Dec 2024)
- **Cause Codes:** ICD-10 underlying causes X40–X44, X60–X64, X85, Y10–Y14 define drug-overdose deaths
- **Adjustments:** Statistical “nowcasting” models generate predicted counts to compensate for reporting lags; reported vs. predicted values are both provided for transparency8.


## Key Query Parameters


- Jurisdiction (nation, state, county)
- Time period (month, quarter, rolling 12-month)
- Specific drug classes (e.g., synthetic opioids T40.4, natural/semi-synthetic opioids T40.2)
- Demographics (age, sex, race/ethnicity)
- Data quality metrics (percent pending investigation, completeness)


## Limitations & Interpretive Cautions


- Provisional counts are incomplete and vary by jurisdiction—recent months typically undercount actual deaths
- Comparisons across jurisdictions or drug classes may be misleading if local death investigations lag differently
- Percentage changes are more reliable when based on predicted counts rather than raw reported counts.


# Deep Dive: DOJ Sanctuary Jurisdiction Directives³


## Legal Foundation & Scope


On February 5, 2025, Attorney General Bondi issued a memorandum defining “sanctuary jurisdictions” as those that refuse to comply with 8 U.S.C. § 1373(a) or other federal immigration laws. The directive leverages the DOJ’s grant-making authority to pressure compliance and threatens enforcement actions against non-cooperative jurisdictions.


## Section I: Funding Conditions for State & Local Jurisdictions


- **Requirement:** All DOJ grants must condition eligibility on certification of compliance with § 1373(a)
- **Timeline:**
  - Within 30 days: Associate AG reports which grants will require certification
  - Freeze/distribution pause until certification is confirmed
- **Enforcement:** DOJ will claw back improperly awarded funds and tailor future awards to “promote a lawful system of immigration”.


## Section II: Review of NGO Agreements


- **Identification:** Components must list all contracts, grants, or agreements with NGOs aiding removable or undocumented aliens
- **60-Day Pause:** Funding halted while NGOs certify that no DOJ funds were used to facilitate immigration-law violations
- **Outcome:** DOJ may terminate or resume specific agreements based on compliance and fraud/waste findings.


## Section III: Pursuit of Enforcement Actions


- **Criminal Prosecution:** U.S. Attorneys to investigate and prosecute obstructions of federal immigration directives (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 371, 8 U.S.C. § 1324)
- **Civil Challenges:** DOJ Civil Division to sue state/local laws, policies, and practices that impede lawful federal immigration operations
- **Reporting:** Declination of any enforcement action must be reported per Justice Manual § 1-13.130.


## Early Impacts & Litigation

# Automating Seizure & Overdose Data Integration


This workflow leverages the FBI’s Crime Data Explorer (CDE) API to pull monthly local drug‐seizure volumes and CDC WONDER’s provisional overdose mortality data for nitazene analogs (“Nitazenes”). The result is a dynamic, geocoded time‐series overlay that highlights where and when emerging synthetics like “New Jack City” drive OD spikes.


## 1. Data Pipeline Architecture


1. **FBI CDE API (Seizures by Agency or County)**
  - Endpoint: `https://api.usa.gov/crime/fbi/sapi/api/estimates/nibrs/agencies/{ORI}/offenses/drug_seizures/{start_year}/{end_year}`
  - Parameters:
    - ORI code or state/county FIPS
    - Offense code for drug seizures
    - Date range each month
  - Response: JSON with monthly counts, drug categories, weights.
2. **CDC WONDER Provisional Overdose Module**
  - Query via HTTP POST to WONDER’s API for “Multiple Cause of Death” (ICD-10 X40–X44, X60–X64, X85, Y10–Y14), filtered by “benzimidazoles (T40.4 analogs)”.
  - Returns rolling 12-month provisional counts by county and month.
3. **ETL & Storage**
  - **Ingest** both feeds nightly into a relational DB or cloud data warehouse.
  - **Transform**:
    - Normalize location codes (FIPS → ORI)
    - Align dates to month boundaries
    - Tag seizure records by synthetic class (e.g., nitazene, fentanyl, heroin).
4. **Visualization & Alerts**
  - Use GIS to overlay county‐level seizure volumes (graduated circles) with nitazene OD trajectories (heatmap).
  - Trigger alerts when a county’s nitazene‐related ODs increase > 20% MoM while seizures of designer synthetics decline.

```
import requests
import pandas as pd

# 1. Pull FBI CDE data
url_cde = "https://api.usa.gov/crime/fbi/sapi/api/estimates/nibrs/agencies/MD0000100/offenses/drug_seizures/2023/2025"
params = {"API_KEY": "YOUR_KEY"}
resp = requests.get(url_cde, params=params).json()
df_seizures = pd.json_normalize(resp['results'])

# 2. Pull CDC WONDER provisional overdoses
url_wonder = "https://wonder.cdc.gov/controller/datarequest/D76"
payload = {
  "stage": 2,
  "action-Send": "Send",
  "request[__report]": "Multiple Cause of Death–Provisional",
  "request[VARS][underlying_cause]": ["X40","X44"],
  "request[VARS][year]": ["2023","2024","2025"],
  "request[VARS][county]": ["24005"],  # Baltimore City
  "request[VARS][month]": ["01","02","03",...],
}
resp_od = requests.post(url_wonder, data=payload)
df_od = pd.read_html(resp_od.text)[0]

# 3. Merge & visualize
df = df_seizures.merge(df_od, left_on=['month','county'], right_on=['Month','County'])
```

## 4. DTO Cartel Supply: Exploiting Sanctuary Cities


- **Jurisdictional Arbitrage**: Cartels reroute shipments toward broad‐sanctuary counties (e.g., Cook, SF, Kings) to exploit ICE non‐cooperation.
- **Cellular Networks**: Mexican cartels (Sinaloa, CJNG) embed modular cells in sanctuary locales, using encrypted apps and cash couriers.
- **Designer Synthetics**: “New Jack City”—a bespoke nitazene cohort 50× more potent than fentanyl—is pushed as premium, often cut with heroin/fentanyl for “Zombie” effects that deter users from testing supply (fear of adulterants).
- **Supply Chain**:
  - **International Labs**: Precursor chemicals shipped via express mail from Asia to Midwest forging labs (Brazil, US, Canada) dismantled in Operation Zeneth.
  - **Domestic Distribution**: Lab‐to‐cell pipeline: Chicago area → SF Bay area → East Coast sanctuary hubs → regional open‐air markets.

## 6. Key Insights & Recommendations


- **ICE Carve-Outs**: Safeguard health service funding from § 1373(a) mandates to keep clinics ICE-free zones in sanctuaries.
- **Mobile Harm Reduction**: Deploy syringe vans, naloxone drop-boxes, and community navigators into high‐seizure sanctuaries.
- **Real-Time Analytics**:
  - Integrate CDE & WONDER with N-DEx alerts for sudden seizures of novel analogs.
  - Use anomaly detection to pre-empt emerging hotspot ODs.
- **Forensic Upgrades**: Equip local labs with UHPLC-MS/MS and Raman spectrometers calibrated for benzimidazole cores.
- **Comparative Research**: Implement difference-in-differences studies between adjacent sanctuary vs. non-sanctuary counties to isolate policy impacts on nitazene OD risk.
