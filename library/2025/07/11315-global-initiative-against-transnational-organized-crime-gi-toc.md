# Global Initiative Report on Kush: Scale, Health Impact, International Dynamics, and Recommendations


## Key Findings


Kush is a **highly addictive and lethal synthetic drug** that has wreaked havoc across West Africa, with Sierra Leone as its epicentre. Since 2022, an estimated **thousands of fatalities** have been linked to kush use in Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Senegal and The Gambia2. In April 2024, the presidents of Sierra Leone and Liberia declared national or public health emergencies over kush, an unprecedented response to a drug crisis in the region.


Chemical analysis conducted by the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime (GI-TOC) in cooperation with national authorities found that roughly **50% of tested kush samples contained nitazenes**, potent synthetic opioids up to 25 times stronger than fentanyl, and the remainder contained synthetic cannabinoids such as MDMB-4en-PINACA commonly seen in European markets. GI-TOC and Clingendael’s research indicates that key precursors for these active ingredients are being imported via maritime routes, air cargo and postal services from China, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom.


The kush market has **rapidly fragmented** since 2019, shifting from a few large networks to a decentralized structure comprising multiple actors—owners, intermediaries (“locks”), importers, “cooks”, distributors and retailers—each capturing distinct profit margins within the value chain. This fragmentation complicates enforcement and suggests the infiltration of some state-embedded actors into protection systems for kush networks.


## Public Health Impact


### Sierra Leone


In Sierra Leone, kush has triggered a monumental strain on public health infrastructure. The country’s only psychiatric hospital reported a **4,000% increase in admissions** for drug addiction since 2020, driven largely by kush use. Overdose fatalities overwhelmed mortuaries, prompting emergency **group cremations** in 2022 and bodies abandoned in streets for authorities to collect early each morning.


Users describe kush’s effects as a “zombie” high—extreme sedation, catatonia, sores and necrotic skin lesions. Some victims lose the ability to walk, while malnutrition and self-neglect lead to multi-organ failure. Sierra Leone’s Director of Mental Health reports that nearly **60% of addicts** admitted to the new detox centre dropped out of school or lost employment due to kush dependency.


### Liberia


Liberia declared kush a **public health emergency** in January 2024, closely following Sierra Leone’s lead. President Boakai’s state-of-the-nation address committed to a **multisectoral task force** integrating health, law enforcement and social services to tackle kush abuse6.


Reports from community health workers in Liberia indicate **dozens of weekly fatalities** and widespread mental health deterioration among users. The epidemic disproportionately affects vulnerable youth in informal settlements, exacerbating crime, unemployment and family breakdown.


### Regional West Africa


Since first entering the region in Sierra Leone in 2016, kush has spread to **five neighbouring countries**—Liberia, Guinea, the Gambia, Guinea-Bissau and Senegal—with seizures reported in Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire by 2024. Official death statistics are sparse, but hospital-based counts in Guinea estimate **35 deaths in 2023** and **18 in 2024**, likely underreporting the true toll due to weak surveillance systems and stigma in care-seeking.


The public health fallout includes surges in:


- **Respiratory infections** and lung embolisms due to smoked nitazenes
- **Skin ulcerations** and severe limb oedema from synthetic opioid use
- **Mental health crises**—paranoia, psychosis, suicidal ideation
- **Criminal behaviours**—theft and exploitation to fund addiction


## International Dynamics


### Supply Chain Dynamics – China’s Role


China is a primary origin for **nitazene precursors**—benzimidazole opioids that fuel kush’s lethality. Research by the U.S. Colombo Plan indicates global flows of nitazenes, with China as a leading source for compounds like protonitazene and metonitazene, frequently seized in international express mail shipments to the U.S. and Canada.


GI-TOC testing confirms kush samples in Sierra Leone contain nitazenes imported via maritime routes and postal services from China, often misdeclared as agricultural chemicals or industrial solvents. China’s **export control gaps** allow dual-use chemicals to enter illicit supply chains, undermining regional and global counter-narcotics efforts.


### Supply Chain Dynamics – United Kingdom’s Role


Ingredients resembling those found in kush have surfaced in postal packages from the UK. Sky News inspected a UK-origin shipping container at Freetown’s port, discovering Amazon UK bags of **acetone** and other solvents used in kush synthesis. Although the UK exports mainly synthetic cannabinoids, GI-TOC cautions that it remains unclear if nitazenes are also being shipped from the UK or only cannabinoids, demanding **disrupted supply chain scrutiny**.


### Supply Chain Dynamics – Netherlands’ Role


The Netherlands is implicated as a **major supplier** of kush precursors. Clingendael’s report and NL Times indicate that the **largest single kush shipment** intercepted in Sierra Leone—**300 kilograms** of “organic material” destined for kush production—originated from Rotterdam in 202411. Dutch criminal networks have established ties with West African distributors, leveraging Rotterdam’s port and global mail routes to export key elements for kush synthesis.


### Trafficking Routes and Logistics


International trafficking routes to Sierra Leone mirror those for other illicit goods: maritime shipping via Rotterdam, Shanghai and Singapore, and postal courier services from Asia and Europe. Detailed Kpler analysis flags intermediary hubs—Hong Kong, the United Arab Emirates, Malaysia and Vietnam—used for precursor shipments before final transit to West Africa.


Within Sierra Leone, distribution relies on **localized “cooks”** who mix imported precursors with marshmallow leaf, creating a retail-ready product sold via **locks**, distributors, and street-level vendors in Freetown’s slums and peri-urban ghettos. This decentralized model enables rapid adaptation to enforcement pressure, as new synthesis nodes can emerge with minimal infrastructure.


## Market Structure and Key Actors


| Actor Type | Role | Profit Margin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Owners** | Finance large import shipments and coordinate networks | Highest, 40–60% |
| **Locks** | Intermediaries linking owners to cooks and distributors | 15–25% |
| **Importers** | Clear containers at ports, maintain maritime/postal logistics | 10–15% |
| **Cooks** | Synthesize kush by mixing active ingredients with base material | 5–10% |
| **Distributors** | Supply retail vendors and protect local markets | 5–8% |
| **Retailers** | Street-level sales to users | 2–5% |
| **Source Countries** | China, Netherlands, UK (export precursors/active ingredients) | Not applicable; regulatory liability |


This multilayer chain underscores the **complexity** and **resilience** of the kush economy, challenging one-size-fits-all responses and requiring **targeted disruption** at multiple nodes.


## International Coordination and Policy Response


### Regional Collaboration: ECOWAS Initiative


In mid-2025, the ECOWAS Parliament launched a **region-wide “war on kush” campaign**, with sensitisation outreach in Freetown’s parliament building and commitments to harmonise policies across member states14. The initiative aims to integrate legislative reforms, public awareness drives, and support for vulnerable communities, leveraging Sierra Leone’s leadership as ECOWAS chair.


### National Legislation and Enforcement


- **Sierra Leone**: National Drug Control Act 2008 criminalizes possession and supply, with penalties up to 14 years for large-scale trafficking and 5 years for possession. Security forces report **60% disruption** of kush supply chains through targeted raids and arrests (SLP, April 2024).
- **Liberia**: National emergency declared Jan 2024, with establishment of **multisectoral steering committee** including ministries of Health, Justice, Youth and Sports, and the Liberia Drug Enforcement Agency to coordinate drug control and public health responses6.


### International Enforcement Gaps


While countries like China have strengthened controls on fentanyl classes since 2019, inconsistent enforcement and lack of data sharing hinder effective precursor regulation. The U.S. and EU’s early warning systems detected nitazenes only after 2019, with hotspots in Estonia, Lithuania and Sweden—underscoring the **global spread** of these opioids beyond North America.


## Recommendations


### 1. Build an Evidence-Based Response


- **Enhance Monitoring and Testing**: Establish a **regional laboratory network** for early detection of kush samples, leveraging existing National Drug Law Enforcement Agency capacities and international partnerships to standardise testing protocols and data sharing.
- **Expand Early Warning Systems**: Integrate kush-specific indicators into ECOWAS’s drug observatory and UNODC’s World Drug Report analytics, ensuring real-time surveillance of market trends and mortality data.


### 2. Disrupt Supply Chains


- **Target Source Countries**: Coordinate with China, the Netherlands and the UK to **tighten export controls** on nitazene and cannabinoid precursors. Implement mandatory end-user certificates and cargo scanning for high-risk shipments destined for West Africa, building on Kpler’s risk profiling of fertilizer and solvent shipments.
- **Strengthen Port and Postal Inspections**: Deploy risk-based profiling and portable detection units at Freetown, Monrovia, Conakry and Bissau ports to intercept kush ingredients. Train customs and postal officials on identifying suspicious packaging patterns, including Amazon-branded bags noted in Freetown seizures.


### 3. Mitigate Harms of Kush Consumption


- **Harm Reduction and Public Education**: Launch community-based harm reduction programs that offer **Naloxone** distribution and overdose response training, informed by GI-TOC’s advocacy for opioid reversal access. Implement school-based prevention curricula highlighting kush dangers.
- **Rehabilitation and Mental Health Services**: Invest in **rehabilitation centres** and expand mental health workforce through training and telepsychiatry partnerships. SSHAP and WHO can co-deploy interdisciplinary teams for peer support and trauma counselling in Freetown and Monrovia.


### 4. Foster Regional Policy Alignment


- **Legislative Reform**: Update National Drug Control Acts to incorporate **decriminalisation of possession for personal use** and emphasise treatment over incarceration, aligning with global public health best practices.
- **Multisectoral Task Forces**: Strengthen ECOWAS and national task forces with clear mandates for coordination among health, criminal justice, customs, education and social welfare sectors, ensuring accountability and resource allocation.


### 5. Empower Local Communities


- **People-Centred Approaches**: Engage youth, former kush users, and community leaders in designing interventions that address underlying socio-economic drivers—unemployment, social exclusion and post-conflict trauma—drawing on SSHAP’s recommendations for holistic responses to the kush epidemic.
- **Alternative Livelihood Programs**: Integrate kush prevention into broader youth employment and skills training initiatives (e.g., Sierra Leone’s FEED SALONE and Youth Employment Scheme), reducing drug vulnerability by increasing economic opportunities.


By confronting kush through **evidence-driven monitoring**, **targeted supply-chain disruption**, **harm reduction**, **regional policy coherence**, and **community empowerment**, West African states, supported by international partners, can begin to reverse the burgeoning synthetic drug crisis and safeguard the health and futures of their youth.
