# Research Report: Framing the HALT Fentanyl Act as a “Plague” in Public Discourse


## 1. Context of the HALT Fentanyl Act


President Trump signed the Halt All Lethal Trafficking (HALT) of Fentanyl Act into law on July 16, 2025, permanently classifying illicit fentanyl-related substances as Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act (CSA)2. This bicameral, bipartisan legislation sailed through the Senate Judiciary Committee led by Chairman Chuck Grassley (R-Iowa) and Senators Bill Cassidy (R-La.) and Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.), and passed the House 321-104, sending it to the president’s desk2.


The Act responds to a stark rise in synthetic opioid overdoses, which claimed over 107,000 lives nationwide in the year ending August 2022, a public health emergency first declared under Executive Order 14059 and renewed in December 2024 to address global illicit drug trafficking, including fentanyl and its analogues. Fentanyl seizures at U.S. borders skyrocketed—CBP reported more than 260,000 pounds in one year—and nearly 90 percent of these seizures occurred at legal ports of entry, although cartels exploit both official crossings and remote “cat-and-mouse” routes to flood the U.S. with white-powder pills that can kill with just two milligrams.


The HALT Act’s core provision amends Schedule I of CSA § 202(c) to include any material containing a fentanyl-related substance defined by specific structural modifications to the fentanyl molecule, leaving no carve-out for research opioids or non-bioactive analogues7. Temporary scheduling of 17 analogues since 2015 demonstrated the “Whac-A-Mole” challenge of reactive controls, motivating permanent, class-wide scheduling to close loopholes that allowed cartel chemists to tweak one molecular bond and evade prosecution.


## 2. Implications of the “Plague” Framing


### 2.1 Public Policy and Enforcement


Framing fentanyl as a “plague” or “scourge” justifies extraordinary policy responses. Senators and governors alike have used the term “plague” to describe the national emergency, suggesting a bio-threat paradigm rather than a public health‐oriented approach that prioritizes harm reduction and treatment over criminalization . By embedding all fentanyl analogues into Schedule I, the Act imposes mandatory minimum sentences—even trace amounts in a mixture carry five-year minimum terms—raising civil rights concerns about mass incarceration and racial disparities in enforcement.


### 2.2 Medical Research and Public Health


Permanent classification of fentanyl‐related substances in Schedule I raises alarms about stifling scientific research on novel opioids for pain and addiction treatment. Critics—including NIDA and numerous scientific societies—warn that broad chemical structure scheduling could freeze out potentially therapeutic compounds and hinder the development of new analgesics or overdose antidotes .


Concurrently, opponents note the Act fails to create an “off-ramp” for rescheduling harmless or beneficial analogues, unlike other bills (e.g., the TEST Act or SAFE Act) that include mechanisms for scientist-driven reevaluation . The lack of an explicit rescheduling process risks prosecuting innocent researchers and cold-case “gray market” compounds that pose no imminent public safety hazard.


### 2.3 Criminal Justice Consequences


Civil rights groups, including Human Rights Watch and the Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights, lament that the Act doubles down on failed “war on drugs” punitive measures, risking further expansion of mandatory minimums and replicating the crack cocaine era’s disproportionate impact on communities of color 10. A single distraught pill laced with fentanyl can carry the same prison term as intentional traffickers, undermining judicial discretion and potentially trapping non-violent users in lengthy sentences.


## 3. Rhetoric Analysis of “Plague” and Fear Appeals


### 3.1 Political Rhetoric and Metaphor


The “plague” metaphor has been prominent in congressional debate. House Oversight Chairman James Comer (R-Ky.) repeatedly described fentanyl as a “plague” upon American families, equating drug deaths to a public health catastrophe demanding urgent legislative action . In the Senate, state attorneys general pushed the Act as ending the “cat and mouse” game with Mexican cartels and Chinese chemical suppliers, reinforcing a narrative of an external invader destroying communities .


Former President Trump amplified this frame at the signing ceremony, hailing the bill as delivering “another defeat for the savage drug smugglers and criminals and the cartels” and framing it as part of a broader crisis response akin to a war footing .


### 3.2 Media Narratives and Fear Appeals


Mainstream media coverage underscored the raw human toll to stoke public fear. CBS 60 Minutes warned the U.S. is “losing a generation” to fentanyl overdoses, and feature segments highlighted 100,000 overdose deaths per year as surpassing battlefield casualties, framing the crisis in terms of existential national security threat . The visuals of family members at signing events, tearful pleas in Fox News reports, and graphic documentaries in local news reinforce fear appeals, relying on emotional salience to spur public support for tough enforcement measures .


From a communications theory perspective, fear appeals—messages that emphasize the dire consequences of fentanyl use—seek to elevate risk perception and drive support for punitive or preventive policies. However, research shows that fear appeals function best when coupled with efficacy messages that offer clear actions audiences can take, such as naloxone training or calling for policy reforms. Absent such guidance, fear can produce defensive avoidance or “risk denial” among target audiences .


### 3.3 Framing Framework


According to Entman’s framing theory, frames work by selecting and highlighting aspects of reality, diagnosing causes, making moral judgments, and suggesting remedies . The HALT Act’s dominant frame identifies Chinese and Mexican cartel chemists as “culprits,” condemns “fentanyl-related substances” as illegitimate drugs with no medical purpose, and prescribes mandatory minimum sentencing as the remedy. This frame effectively narrows discourse away from public health approaches—such as expanded treatment, safe consumption spaces, and syringe exchange—and channels it toward law enforcement solutions .


## 4. Case Studies of Public Discourse


- **Grassley Senate Press Release**: “The HALT Fentanyl Act is now the law of the land, marking a major victory in America’s fight against fentanyl,” invoked a victory frame, pitting the Act against “the Biden administration’s disastrous policies” for political gain .
- **Human Rights Watch Letter**: Over 150 public health, civil rights, and medical organizations warned H.R. 467 would “stifle research” and that “harmless substances” could be swept into Schedule I, framing the Act as a threat to scientific innovation and civil liberties .
- **Leadership Conference Statement**: The group criticized the Act as “punitive” and “ineffective,” urging a shift to “health-based approaches” rather than more enforcement, reframing the issue as one of medical treatment rather than criminal law .
- **Families’ Advocacy**: Groups like Families Against Fentanyl and individual testimonies from grieving parents such as Erin Rachwal and Greg Swan put a human face on the “plague,” wielding emotional testimony to bolster the moral imperative for action 11.


## 5. Conclusions and Recommendations


The HALT Fentanyl Act’s framing as a “plague” has mobilized bipartisan support for permanent scheduling of fentanyl analogues, but this law-enforcement-centric frame risks sidelining public health and research solutions. Fear and “plague” metaphors heighten risk salience but must be balanced with efficacy messages—such as expanded treatment access, naloxone distribution, and syringe services—to avoid defensive avoidance. Communications scholars, policymakers, and practitioners should:


1. Reframe the response to emphasize **harm reduction** and **treatment** alongside enforcement, citing evidence that MAT and overdose prevention center programs reduce fatalities more than punitive measures alone.
2. Incorporate **efficacy messages** into media campaigns to ensure fear appeals do not engender denial or reactance.
3. **Monitor research** on Schedule I class effects, advocating for an “off-ramp” to reschedule harmless or therapeutic analogues, as proposed in competing bills like the TEST Act.
4. **Evaluate** public opinion dynamics linking “plague” narratives to support for health policy, using Zaller’s framing insights to test which frames produce durable, positive behavior change without collateral stigmatization.


By broadening the dominant frame beyond “plague” and “cat-and-mouse,” Congress and the media can craft a more nuanced narrative that integrates enforcement with evidence-based public health strategies, fostering resilience in individuals, communities, and systems as we combat the opioid crisis.
