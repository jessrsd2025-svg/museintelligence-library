***Nitazenes: a chemical plague mirroring historical outbreaks***


The rapid and clandestine proliferation of nitazenes, a class of potent synthetic opioids, has prompted comparisons to historical biological pandemics like the Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone (2014-2016) and the 1854 cholera outbreak in London. These comparisons are based on several shared characteristics, including the rapid spread of the threat, high fatality rates, and the need for a multi-faceted public health response.


**Parallels between nitazenes and historical outbreaks**


**1. Mode of spread**


- **Nitazenes:** Propagate through illicit networks, often masked in other substances, making their presence difficult to detect and contributing to their stealthy emergence.
- **Ebola (Sierra Leone 2014-2016):** Spread through direct contact with infected individuals and their bodily fluids, presenting challenges in containing the virus, especially in communities with limited resources and established cultural practices that involved touching the deceased.
- **Cholera (London 1854):** Spread through contaminated water sources, highlighting the critical role of environmental factors in disease transmission.


**2. High fatality rates**


- **Nitazenes:** The extreme potency of nitazenes, often exceeding that of fentanyl, significantly increases the risk of fatal respiratory depression in acute overdoses.
- **Ebola:** The 2014-2016 Ebola outbreak in West Africa was the deadliest occurrence of the disease, killing five times more than all other known Ebola outbreaks combined. Case fatality rates for Ebola have ranged from 25-90%, while the 2013-2016 outbreak had a lower case-fatality rate compared to other 24 outbreaks.
- **Cholera:** The 1854 cholera outbreak in London’s Golden Square neighborhood killed 1 in 10 inhabitants over 10 days, according to a C-SPAN report. The historical case fatality rate for cholera, particularly before the 20th century, was also extraordinarily high.


3.**Detection challenges**


- **Nitazenes:** Often go undetected in standard toxicology screens, posing significant challenges for surveillance and forensic drug testing, according to the NIH HEAL Initiative. This necessitates the development and distribution of new analytical methods and tools to identify novel substances in the drug supply.
- **Ebola:** Recognition of Ebola virus infection may be delayed in the absence of specific molecular diagnostic tests, leading to additional transmission and delayed appropriate medical care, says the NIH.
- **Cholera:** In the 1850s, cholera was believed to be spread by miasma in the air, hindering the understanding of its true cause of transmission. Dr. John Snow’s work in mapping cholera deaths played a pivotal role in identifying the contaminated water pump as the source of the outbreak, ushering in new approaches to epidemiological investigation.


4. **Required public health responses**


All three outbreaks highlight the need for a multifaceted public health response, including:


- **Enhanced Surveillance:** Rapid detection and reporting of cases are essential to track the spread of the threat and implement timely interventions.
- **Clinical Management:** Development of updated clinical protocols and effective treatments for the respective threats is crucial for improving patient outcomes.
- **Harm Reduction/Prevention:** Implementing strategies to prevent further spread, such as naloxone distribution for nitazenes, safe consumption sites, infection control measures for Ebola, and improving water and sanitation infrastructure for cholera, are critical.
- **Community Engagement:** Engaging communities and building trust are vital for successful interventions and ensuring compliance with public health measures.
- **Global Collaboration:** International cooperation and coordinated efforts among various stakeholders are essential for tackling these threats effectively.


**The need for a proactive and coordinated response**


The nitazene crisis requires a pandemic-style response. This includes accelerated surveillance, updated clinical protocols, and global collaboration in interdiction efforts. Lessons from the Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone and the 1854 cholera outbreak in London highlight the need for a sustained, multidisciplinary strategy to detect, treat, and prevent nitazene-related harm. Learning from these historical events emphasizes the importance of early warning, rapid testing, and coordinated action across public health, clinical medicine, and law enforcement. Without immediate, evidence-driven interventions, this synthetic opioid plague may cause decades of additional illness and death.
