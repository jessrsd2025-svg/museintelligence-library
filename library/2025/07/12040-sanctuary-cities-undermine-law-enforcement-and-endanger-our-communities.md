## Sanctuary Policies in the U.S.


Sanctuary policies vary, but generally **limit local government cooperation with federal immigration enforcement**, especially regarding detainment/deportation of undocumented immigrants unless very serious crimes are involved. Examples include:


### Major Cities and Jurisdictions


- **San Francisco, CA:**
  - *“Due Process for All”* ordinance; local law enforcement cannot hold people solely for ICE requests without judicial warrant.
- **Chicago, IL:**
  - *Welcoming City Ordinance*; bars city employees and police from questioning individuals about immigration status or cooperating with ICE except in specific violent crime cases.
- **Los Angeles, CA:**
  - *Special Order 40*; LAPD does not initiate police action with the goal of identifying individuals solely on immigration status.
- **Seattle, WA; Denver, CO; Boston, MA; Philadelphia, PA** (and counties like King County, WA and Cook County, IL):
  - Prohibit honoring ICE detainers without a judicial warrant.
  - Refuse to share jail release dates or allow ICE interviews without consent.
  - Forbid use of local tax dollars for federal immigration enforcement.

- **California:**
  - *California Values Act (SB 54):* Police generally cannot detain, question, or arrest people for immigration enforcement purposes.
- **Oregon:**
  - *1987 State Law* barring police from using resources to enforce federal immigration law.

## Executive Summary


The relationship between U.S. sanctuary policy architecture and transnational drug trafficking organizations (DTOs) is complex and consequential, fueling persistent open-air drug markets and ongoing public health crises. This report explores:


- The structure and logic of sanctuary policies across major U.S. jurisdictions.

## I. Sanctuary Policy Landscape in the U.S.


### 1.1 Sanctuary Policy: Definition and Rationale


Sanctuary policies are laws, resolutions, or practices adopted by states, counties, or cities to **limit local law enforcement’s participation in federal immigration enforcement** (DHS/ICE), particularly where civil immigration infractions are involved. The rationale includes:


- Promoting community trust to encourage reporting of crimes.
- Preventing racial profiling.
- Focusing local policing resources on threats to public safety, not civil immigration matters.


### 1.2 Major Sanctuary Jurisdictions: Policies and Practice

## 2. How Do Economic Factors Influence DTO Networks?


### A. Labor Supply & Incentives


- **Poverty/limited opportunity** (in origin countries—e.g., Honduras, DR, Mexico, Guatemala): → Creates a steady supply of *willing recruits* for DTOs, who offer far higher earnings than local alternatives.
- **Migrant labor flows:** → Both legal and unauthorized migration routes are exploited for *recruitment, infiltration, and recycling* of street-level traffickers. → “New faces” (no criminal record) are harder to detect and prosecute.


### B. U.S. Market Dynamics


- **High Income/Low Entry Barriers:**
  - DTOs concentrate in urban centers where street-level dealing can bring high-profit margins per capita (e.g., Bronx, Tenderloin, Kensington, South LA).
- **Open-Air Markets:**
  - Persist in low-income/high-unemployment districts where local economies are weak and enforcement is under-resourced.
- **Retail Adaptation:**
  - DTOs shift retail personnel, locations, and sales methods in response to local economics—adapting pop-up sites, delivery, or more overt “open air” sales when enforcement is weak or community distrust of police is high.


### C. Policy Effects on DTO Economics


- **Sanctuary policies reduce personnel loss costs:**
  - Replacement via international labor pool is cheaper/less risky (less ICE removal).
- **Supply chain optimization:**
  - DTOs adapt, shifting supply and distribution routes in response to law enforcement or market shocks (e.g., after a major bust, another supply corridor is emphasized).


### D. Consumer Economics


- **Demand surges** (e.g., economic instability, COVID unemployment, prescription opioid crackdowns) → Increase demand for street opioids/stimulants → Makes DTO distribution even more profitable—fuels expansion, not contraction.
